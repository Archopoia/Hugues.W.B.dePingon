# The Problem of Abstraction

## The Question

Abstraction is one of the most powerful things the human mind does. It lets us take a thousand different chairs and hold them all in one small word. But every power has a shadow, and I want to look at the shadow of this one. What happens when an abstraction becomes so familiar that we forget it is an abstraction at all?

## The Main Idea

My claim is simple: modern concepts tend to drift away from the experiences that produced them, and once they drift far enough we start to treat them as things. We reify them. We speak of "the economy," "society," "the self," "justice," as though they were objects we could bump into, rather than compressions we made to get through the day. The trouble is not that we abstract. The trouble is that we forget we are doing it.

## The Explanation

Every abstraction begins as a shortcut across experience. Someone noticed a pattern - repeated exchanges, repeated fairness, repeated feelings of being a continuous person - and gave the pattern a name. The name was a convenience, a handle. Handles are good. You cannot carry water without one.

But a handle used long enough starts to feel like the water. This is reification: treating a concept as if it had the same solidity as a stone. Once "intelligence" is a thing, we can measure it, rank it, and hand out fates according to it. Once "the market" is a thing, it can *want* and *punish* and *decide*. The abstraction quietly acquires the authority of a fact.

The cost shows up in two ways.

First, we stop being able to revise. A shortcut can be redrawn when the terrain changes; a supposed object resists change, because objects are not supposed to be up to us. Reified concepts become prisons precisely because we no longer remember building them.

Second, we lose contact with the concrete. Whole conversations can be conducted entirely among abstractions, each defined only by other abstractions, none of them ever touching down in anything you could see or feel. It can sound rigorous. It can also be a closed loop with no exit to the world.

There is a discipline that helps: keep asking what experience a word was built to compress. Not to abolish the word - we need it - but to remember its origin, and so to keep the right to remake it.

## Conclusion

So here is the question I keep, without pretending to have finished it: can we communicate complex truths while staying tethered to concrete reality? I think we can, but only if we treat our abstractions as tools rather than discoveries - useful compressions we are always free to unpack.

An abstraction remembered as an abstraction is a gift. An abstraction mistaken for a thing is a quiet trap.
